# Selection rubric

A scoring guide for reviewers selecting the cohort, with a method for small pools and one for large pools.

**When:** Finalize at T-7w; reviewers score from [Application deadline] (about T-6w); selection meeting at T-5w, before decision emails go out.

**Sent by / owner:** Program organizer, shared with all reviewers (Team Leads and Company mentors).

**Audience:** Reviewers on the selection team.

## Template

### Pick a method by pool size

**Small pools (roughly up to [N] applicants, or when applicants are few relative to seats): live criteria meeting**
- Block one meeting with all reviewers before reading applications.
- Clean the responses and group candidates by likely project.
- Review together in one live tracker, discussing each applicant against these criteria:
  - Motivation and clarity of interest
  - Availability during the series window
  - Alignment with the supported projects
  - Mentor capacity for the chosen project
  - Likelihood to follow through
- Mark each person: Accept / Waitlist / Not selected / Needs follow-up. Record the reason, especially when mentor capacity is the deciding factor.

**Large pools: independent scoring, then a gray-zone meeting**
- Assign each application to at least [N] reviewers. Each reviewer scores 1-10 independently, without seeing others' scores.
- Consolidate: average score, total, and variance per applicant.
- Clear accepts (high average, low variance) and clear declines (low average, low variance) move forward without discussion.
- Hold a gray-zone meeting for applicants near the cutoff, those with high score variance, and anyone flagged for group discussion.
- Finalize placements against project caps, then build the waitlist in rank order per project.

### What to look for (weigh holistically, in no strict order)

- **Real use of the projects, with a specific target.** Applicants who use the tools, ideally in their day job, and name something specific they want to fix, improve, or document. Specific, in-scope ideas are one of the strongest signals of who sticks around.
- **Open source experience or excitement.** Prior public open source contributions count, and so does genuine excitement from someone new.
- **The real-issue answer.** How they'd approach a real issue they picked shows follow-through and how they think, better than self-ratings. Look for a sensible first step and a willingness to ask, not a perfect solution.
- **Effort and specificity in the application.** Did they engage with the questions? A thoughtful, specific answer predicts how someone will show up. This often matters most.
- **Interest in community engagement.** Interest in joining project chat, community meetings, triage, reviews, or answering questions, not only writing code. This predicts people who keep showing up.
- **Community involvement.** Attending the Setup Sprint is a meaningful signal of follow-through. Membership in an employee community or guild is a positive sign but not decisive.
- **Commitment.** Can they give about [N] hours a week and attend office hours?

### Score bands (use judgment, not a formula)

- **9-10:** Strong on all: uses the tools with a specific target, strong open source experience or excitement, and a thoughtful application
- **6-8:** Solid on 2-3 of the above; a good fit even if not a standout everywhere
- **3-5:** Meets the bar but thin on real use, motivation, or application effort
- **1-2:** Does not meet the commitment bar, or answers are too vague or generic to assess

### Guardrails

- **Flag personal connections.** If you know an applicant or have a network connection, note it and flag them for group discussion rather than letting it shape one reviewer's score.
- **Don't score prior participation up or down.** The organizer brings past-participant engagement (for example, public activity from previous cohorts) into the group discussion as context.
- **Usage alone never earns 7+.** Using the tools is not enough; a 7 or above needs a specific, in-scope target or clear evidence of how they'll engage.
- **Judge public open source only.** Internal or inner-source work is part of someone's day job and doesn't count as open source experience. Any public profile counts, including one that looks like a work identity.
- **Protect small projects.** Deliberately place strong applicants in smaller or less-requested projects so every project has a viable group.
- **Cap popular projects by reviewer capacity.** Set a cap per project based on how many participants its mentors and maintainers can actually support, and stick to it.
- **Write a note for extreme scores.** Add a short reason for every 1-2 and 9-10 score.

### Reviewer notes for strong applicants

- Recommend a primary and secondary project placement.
- Note anything that would help the group decide a borderline case.

### Tracker columns

- Applicant ID, first choice, second choice, one score column per reviewer, average, variance, combined notes, flags (personal connection, regional cohort, needs follow-up), recommended placement, final decision

## How to use

- Choose the method by pool size before the deadline and block meeting time with all reviewers in advance.
- Share this rubric and the response sheet (restricted to reviewers) at the same time; give reviewers a scoring deadline before the meeting.
- Set per-project caps from mentor capacity before scoring starts, not after.
- If you run a regional cohort, route those applicants to the regional mentors with your scores as input; let them make their own decisions.
- Keep notes factual and respectful; applicants' personal data stays in the restricted sheet.

## Notes for alignment

- **Recommendation:** Public. The method and guardrails are company-neutral; companies only adjust signals like employee community programs.
- **Company-specific parts:** employee community and guild examples, reviewer roster, per-project caps, whether employee IDs are used.
